Short, plain guides to the numbers on Market Check: what they mean, how they are worked out, and where they can mislead you.

Market Check shows a lot of figures on one screen: prices, percentage changes over five different windows, market caps, heat maps, a fear and greed reading, portfolio gains and the day's headlines. Each of them answers a narrow question, and each can be misread. These guides explain what each one is measuring, with worked examples, so you can tell a real move from noise.

They are written for people who follow markets as part of everyday life rather than for a living. None of them tells you what to buy or sell, and nothing here is financial advice.

Reading the dashboard

  • How to read a heat map and a bubble map: what the size and colour of each tile mean, why one giant tile can hide the rest of the market, and the questions a heat map cannot answer.
  • Price changes: 1h, 24h, 1D, 1W, YTD and the rest: how a percentage change is measured, why crypto and stock windows are not the same thing, and why our figure can differ from your broker's.
  • Market cap, explained: how the size of a company, a coin and a fund is measured, circulating supply versus fully diluted value, and why a low price does not make something cheap.
  • The Crypto Fear & Greed Index: what the number on the overview is made from, how often it changes, and how much weight it can bear.

Markets and how they run

  • Stocks, ETFs and indexes: the difference between owning a company, owning a fund and watching an index, and how the S&P 500, the Nasdaq-100 and the Dow are put together.
  • Market hours, holidays and the dates that matter: regular hours, pre-market and after-hours, early closes, Federal Reserve decision days and monthly options expiration, and why crypto never closes.

Your own lists and the news

  • Tracking a portfolio: value, day change and profit or loss: the arithmetic behind the portfolio page, what cost basis means, and what a simple tracker leaves out.
  • Reading market news without being pushed around by it: how to read a market headline, tell reporting from opinion, and avoid the trap of explaining every move after the fact.
