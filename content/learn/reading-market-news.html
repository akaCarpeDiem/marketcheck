Reading market news without being pushed around by it

How to get the most out of the headlines page, and the habits that keep news useful rather than stressful.

The news page collects recent headlines from many publishers and sorts them into ten topics. Each one links to the original story. Market Check does not write or rewrite articles, and a headline appearing here is not an endorsement. That makes the page a good place to see what is being talked about, and a poor place to stop reading. Here are some habits that help.

Read past the headline

Headlines are written to be clicked, and market headlines in particular compress a lot into a few words. "Stocks slide as yields jump" might describe a 0.3% dip. Before reacting, open the story and look for the actual numbers, then compare them with the prices: how big was the move, and over what period?

Tell reporting from opinion

A news report describes what happened, who said it and when. A column or analysis piece argues a view. Both are useful, but they are different. Signs of opinion include a named columnist, words like "should", "will" or "must", and forecasts stated with confidence. Sponsored content and press releases are different again: they are written by or for the company being described.

Be wary of "because"

Daily market stories often explain a move with a single cause: "shares rose because of X". Markets are moved by millions of decisions with many motives, and the explanation is usually written after the move by matching it to the most prominent news of the day. Sometimes the link is clear, such as a company falling sharply right after its results. Often it is a plausible story rather than a proven one. Two things help:

  • Check timing. Did the move happen after the news was published, or before?
  • Check breadth. If the whole market moved, a story about one company is unlikely to be the whole reason. The heat map shows this at a glance.

Compare sources

Because the news page mixes publishers, you will often see the same event covered by several of them. Different outlets emphasise different details, and reading two accounts is a quick way to separate the facts everyone agrees on from the framing. No single publisher fills a topic on Market Check: each is limited to a few stories at a time, and the same story is shown once.

Know what a topic is

Stories are placed in a topic by the feed they came from and by the words in their headline, so occasionally one lands in a neighbouring topic. If a story looks misplaced, it probably is; let us know.

Slow down on big days

On days with large moves, the volume of headlines rises sharply and many are written quickly, with early figures that are later corrected. If you are making a decision that matters, waiting for fuller reporting, or for the official source (a company filing, a central bank statement, a government release), is rarely a mistake.

Podcasts for depth

Headlines tell you what happened; longer conversations are better for why it might matter. The podcast hub is a hand-picked directory of shows filed under the same ten topics. Being listed is not an endorsement of what is said in a show.
